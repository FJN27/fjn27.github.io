Kinematic Equations

For motion with constant acceleration, the following equations are useful:

\(v = v_0 + at\)

\(\Delta x = \frac{v+v_0}{2}t\)

\(\Delta x = v_0t + \frac{1}{2}at^2\)

\(v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a\Delta x\)

Symbols

  • \(v_0\): Initial velocity
  • \(v\): Final velocity
  • \(a\): Acceleration
  • \(t\): Elapsed time
  • \(\Delta x\): Displacement

Vectors and Scalars

A vector is a physical quantity that has both magnitude and direction, whereas a scalar is a physical quantity that has magnitude only. Magnitude describes the size of a quantity.

Example

Consider a ball moving at 3 meters per second to the left.

  • 3 meters per second (3 m/s): The magnitude of the velocity, also known as speed.
  • Left: The direction of motion.
  • 3 meters per second to the left: The velocity, which includes both magnitude and direction.

Motion Diagrams

Motion graphs provide a visual representation of how an object's position, velocity, and acceleration change over time.

In this section, we will explore three types of graphs:

  • Displacement vs. Time
  • Velocity vs. Time
  • Acceleration vs. Time

When interpreting motion graphs, two important concepts are the gradient (slope) and the area under the curve.

1. Displacement vs. Time

Velocity is the rate of change of displacement with respect to time. Therefore, the gradient of a displacement-time graph represents velocity.

For a straight-line graph:

\(v = \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}\)

  • Zero gradient: The object is stationary.
  • Positive gradient: The object moves in the positive direction.
  • Negative gradient: The object moves in the negative direction.
  • Steeper gradient: The object has a greater speed.

A straight line with a constant gradient indicates constant velocity. A curved line indicates changing velocity, and its gradient at a particular point gives the instantaneous velocity.

2. Velocity vs. Time

The gradient of a velocity-time graph represents acceleration:

\(a = \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}\)

  • Zero gradient: The object moves at constant velocity.
  • Positive gradient: The object has positive acceleration.
  • Negative gradient: The object has negative acceleration.

The signed area under a velocity-time graph represents displacement:

\(\Delta x = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} v(t)\,dt\)

  • Area above the time axis contributes positive displacement.
  • Area below the time axis contributes negative displacement.
  • The sum of the absolute areas represents the total distance traveled.

3. Acceleration vs. Time

The signed area under an acceleration-time graph represents the change in velocity:

\(\Delta v = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} a(t)\,dt\)

  • Positive area: Contributes a positive change in velocity.
  • Negative area: Contributes a negative change in velocity.
  • Zero acceleration: The object's velocity remains constant.

The gradient of an acceleration-time graph represents the rate of change of acceleration, known as jerk. This is generally beyond the scope of introductory IB Physics.
