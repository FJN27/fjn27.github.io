Motion in One Dimension

How can we describe the motion of a car, a falling ball, or a person walking along a straight road? In this lesson, we develop the language and mathematical tools of kinematics—the study of motion without considering the forces that cause it. We begin with position and displacement, then explore velocity, acceleration, motion graphs, and the equations of uniformly accelerated motion.

Although real objects can move in three dimensions, many important situations can be described using just one coordinate. Understanding one-dimensional motion provides the foundation for projectile motion, Newton's laws, and more advanced mechanics.

Learning Objectives

After completing this lesson, you should be able to:

  • Distinguish scalar quantities from vector quantities and explain why signs matter in one-dimensional motion.
  • Calculate displacement, distance, average speed, and average velocity.
  • Distinguish average quantities from instantaneous velocity and acceleration.
  • Interpret dot diagrams and position–time, velocity–time, and acceleration–time graphs.
  • Use graph gradients and signed areas to find velocity, acceleration, displacement, and changes in velocity.
  • Derive and apply the constant-acceleration kinematic equations.
  • Analyze vertical motion under gravity using a consistent sign convention.

1. Vectors and Scalars

Every physical quantity has a magnitude (its size), but some quantities also have a direction. A scalar has magnitude only, while a vector has both magnitude and direction. For example, saying a ball travels at 3 m/s describes its speed; saying it travels at 3 m/s to the left describes its velocity.

In one dimension, we can express direction using a positive or negative sign. For example, if we choose right as positive, a velocity of \( +3\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \) means motion to the right, while \( -3\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \) means motion to the left. Neither sign makes the speed negative: speed is always nonnegative.

Key idea: You may choose which direction is positive, but you must use the same convention throughout a calculation.

2. Position, Distance, and Displacement

Position tells us where an object is relative to a chosen origin. We denote its coordinate by \(x\). Displacement measures the change in position between two instants:

\[\Delta x=x_f-x_i\]

Displacement depends only on the initial and final positions, not on the path taken. Distance traveled, by contrast, is the total length of the path. It is always nonnegative.

Worked Example: A Walk Along a Straight Road

A student starts at \(x=0\), walks 8 m to the right, then walks 3 m back to the left. Taking right as positive, the final position is \(x_f=+5\,\mathrm{m}\).

Displacement: \(\Delta x=5-0=+5\,\mathrm{m}\).

Distance: \(8+3=11\,\mathrm{m}\).

The student has traveled 11 m, but ends up only 5 m to the right of the starting point. If the student returned all the way to the start, the displacement would be zero even though the distance traveled would not be.

3. Speed and Velocity

Average speed is total distance traveled divided by elapsed time. Average velocity is displacement divided by elapsed time:

\[\text{average speed}=\frac{\text{total distance}}{\Delta t}\] \[v_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}\]

These quantities can differ whenever an object changes direction. For the student in the previous example, if the walk takes 11 s, the average speed is \(11/11=1.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), but the average velocity is \(+5/11\approx+0.45\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).

Instantaneous Velocity

A speedometer tells you how fast a vehicle is moving at a particular instant, rather than over an entire trip. Mathematically, instantaneous velocity is the limit of average velocity as the time interval becomes very small:

\[v(t)=\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}=\frac{dx}{dt}\]

The instantaneous speed is the magnitude \(|v(t)|\). The derivative notation is useful for interpreting curved position–time graphs; you do not need calculus to solve most introductory constant-acceleration problems.

4. Acceleration

Acceleration describes how quickly velocity changes. An object can accelerate by speeding up, slowing down, or reversing direction. Average acceleration is

\[a_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac{v_f-v_i}{\Delta t}=\frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}\]

The SI unit is \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\): a change in velocity of one metre per second, every second. Instantaneous acceleration is \(a(t)=dv/dt\).

Important: Negative acceleration does not necessarily mean slowing down. If an object moves left (negative velocity) and accelerates left (negative acceleration), its speed increases. Speed decreases when velocity and acceleration have opposite signs.

Worked Example: Slowing to a Stop

A car moving right at \(+20\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) brakes uniformly and stops in 5.0 s. Its acceleration is

\[a=\frac{0-20}{5.0}=-4.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}.\]

The negative sign indicates that the acceleration points left, opposite to the car's velocity. The car slows down because the acceleration opposes its motion.

5. Motion Diagrams

A motion diagram represents an object's position at equally spaced times. Imagine taking a photograph every 0.5 s and marking the object's position with a dot. The spacing between successive dots tells us about its speed.

  • Equal spacing: constant speed (for motion in a single direction).
  • Increasing spacing: the object is speeding up.
  • Decreasing spacing: the object is slowing down.
  • Changing direction: the sequence of dots reverses along the line; direction arrows help distinguish the order of motion.

Motion diagrams are different from motion graphs. A diagram shows snapshots of the object's location, whereas a graph plots a measured quantity against time. Both can describe the same motion from different perspectives.

6. Interpreting Motion Graphs

Motion graphs show how position, velocity, or acceleration changes with time. Two particularly useful ideas are gradient (slope) and signed area. Always check the labels and units on both axes before interpreting a graph.

6.1 Position–Time Graphs

The gradient of a position–time graph gives velocity. For a straight line, the gradient is constant and equals \(\Delta x/\Delta t\). For a curved graph, the tangent gradient at a particular point gives instantaneous velocity.

  • Horizontal line: the object is stationary.
  • Positive gradient: motion in the positive direction.
  • Negative gradient: motion in the negative direction.
  • Steeper gradient in magnitude: greater speed.

For example, if position changes from 2 m to 14 m in 4 s along a straight-line graph, velocity is \((14-2)/4=+3\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). A position–time graph's height is position, not speed.

6.2 Velocity–Time Graphs

The gradient of a velocity–time graph gives acceleration. A horizontal line means constant velocity; a line sloping upward means positive acceleration; and a line sloping downward means negative acceleration.

\[a=\frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}\qquad\Delta x=\text{signed area under the }v\text{–}t\text{ graph}\]

For a horizontal segment, displacement is the rectangular area \(v\Delta t\). For a straight line starting at zero velocity, displacement is the triangular area \(\tfrac12 vt\). Regions below the time axis contribute negative displacement. To find total distance, add the magnitudes of the separate signed regions instead.

Worked Example: Area Under a Velocity–Time Graph

An object moves at \(+4\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) for 3 s, then at \(-2\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) for 2 s. Its displacement is

\[\Delta x=(4)(3)+(-2)(2)=+8\,\mathrm{m}.\]

Its total distance is \(12+4=16\,\mathrm{m}\). Notice that the displacement and distance are not equal because the object changes direction.

6.3 Acceleration–Time Graphs

The signed area under an acceleration–time graph gives the change in velocity:

\[\Delta v=\int_{t_i}^{t_f}a(t)\,dt\]

For constant acceleration, this reduces to \(\Delta v=a\Delta t\). A graph on the time axis (\(a=0\)) means velocity is constant, not necessarily zero. The gradient of an acceleration–time graph is called jerk, which is not normally required for introductory IB Physics.

7. Kinematic Equations for Constant Acceleration

When acceleration is constant, we can connect initial velocity \(v_0\), final velocity \(v\), displacement \(\Delta x\), acceleration \(a\), and elapsed time \(t\) using four equations:

\[v=v_0+at\] \[\Delta x=\frac{v_0+v}{2}t\] \[\Delta x=v_0t+\frac12 at^2\] \[v^2=v_0^2+2a\Delta x\]

Here, \(v_0\) is the velocity at the beginning of the chosen time interval, \(v\) is the velocity at its end, and \(\Delta x=x_f-x_i\). All velocities and displacements are signed quantities.

Where Do These Equations Come From?

Start with the definition of constant acceleration:

\[a=\frac{v-v_0}{t}\quad\Rightarrow\quad v=v_0+at.\]

Because velocity changes linearly with time, the average velocity is the arithmetic mean of the initial and final velocities. Multiplying by time gives

\[\Delta x=\frac{v_0+v}{2}t.\]

Substitute \(v=v_0+at\) into this expression:

\[\begin{aligned}\Delta x&=\frac{v_0+(v_0+at)}{2}t\\&=v_0t+\frac12 at^2.\end{aligned}\]

Finally, eliminating time from \(v=v_0+at\) and \(\Delta x=(v_0+v)t/2\) gives

\[v^2-v_0^2=2a\Delta x\quad\Rightarrow\quad v^2=v_0^2+2a\Delta x.\]

These relationships are not universal: they require constant acceleration over the time interval. If acceleration varies, use a suitable graph, calculus, or another model.

How to Choose an Equation

List the known quantities and identify the unknown. Then select an equation that does not contain another unknown. For example, if time is not given and is not required, \(v^2=v_0^2+2a\Delta x\) is often the simplest choice.

Worked Example: A Bicycle Accelerating

A bicycle moves right at \(2.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) and accelerates uniformly at \(+1.5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) for 4.0 s. Find its final velocity and displacement.

Step 1: Choose right as positive and record \(v_0=+2.0\), \(a=+1.5\), and \(t=4.0\) in SI units.

Step 2: Calculate final velocity:

\[v=2.0+(1.5)(4.0)=+8.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.\]

Step 3: Calculate displacement:

\[\Delta x=(2.0)(4.0)+\frac12(1.5)(4.0)^2=+20\,\mathrm{m}.\]

The bicycle moves 20 m to the right while increasing its speed.

8. Free-Fall Motion

An object is in free fall when gravity is the only force acting on it. Near Earth's surface, gravitational acceleration has approximately constant magnitude \(g=9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) and points downward. When air resistance is negligible, objects of different masses fall with the same acceleration.

For vertical motion, replace \(x\) with the vertical coordinate \(y\). If we choose upward as positive, then \(a_y=-g\):

\[v_y=v_{0y}-gt\] \[\Delta y=v_{0y}t-\frac12 gt^2\] \[v_y^2=v_{0y}^2-2g\Delta y\]

Dropping an Object

If an object is released from rest, \(v_{0y}=0\). Its downward displacement after time \(t\) is \(\Delta y=-\tfrac12 gt^2\). The distance fallen is therefore \(\tfrac12 gt^2\), as long as the object continues downward.

Worked Example: Dropping a Ball

A ball is released from rest and falls for 2.0 s. Ignoring air resistance, find its velocity and vertical displacement. Take upward as positive.

\[v_y=0-(9.81)(2.0)=-19.6\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\] \[\Delta y=0-\frac12(9.81)(2.0)^2=-19.6\,\mathrm{m}\]

The ball is moving downward at about 19.6 m/s and is 19.6 m below its release point. The numerical values happen to be equal here, but velocity and displacement have different units.

Throwing a Ball Upward

A ball thrown upward slows down while rising because its acceleration points downward. At the highest point, its instantaneous vertical velocity is zero, but its acceleration is still \(-g\). It then begins moving downward and gains speed.

Worked Example: Maximum Height

A ball is thrown vertically upward at \(+14.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). Find the time to reach its highest point and the maximum height above the release point, neglecting air resistance.

At the highest point, \(v_y=0\):

\[0=14.0-9.81t\quad\Rightarrow\quad t=1.43\,\mathrm{s}.\]

Using the equation without time:

\[0=(14.0)^2-2(9.81)\Delta y\] \[\Delta y=\frac{196}{19.62}\approx 9.99\,\mathrm{m}.\]

The maximum height is approximately 10.0 m above the launch point.

Common misconception: The acceleration of a ball at the highest point is not zero. Only its instantaneous vertical velocity is zero. Gravity continues to act throughout the flight.

9. Motion on an Inclined Plane: A Preview

Motion along a straight ramp is also one-dimensional if we measure position along the slope. However, determining the acceleration requires an analysis of forces, so inclined-plane dynamics belongs primarily with Newton's laws.

For a frictionless incline at angle \(\theta\) to the horizontal, the component of gravitational acceleration along the slope has magnitude \(g\sin\theta\). If we choose down the slope as positive, \(a=g\sin\theta\). Once the acceleration is known, the same constant-acceleration equations apply along the slope.

With friction or additional forces, acceleration must be determined from the net force; it is not necessarily \(g\sin\theta\).

10. Practice Questions

Try these problems before opening the solutions. State your positive direction, show your equations, and include units.

  1. Distance and displacement: A runner travels 30 m east and then 12 m west. Find the total distance and displacement.
    Show solution

    Distance = \(30+12=42\,\mathrm{m}\). Taking east as positive, displacement = \(+30-12=+18\,\mathrm{m}\), or 18 m east.

  2. Average speed and velocity: The runner completes the journey above in 14 s. Find average speed and average velocity.
    Show solution

    Average speed = \(42/14=3.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). Average velocity = \(18/14\approx1.29\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) east.

  3. Constant acceleration: A car starts from rest and accelerates uniformly at \(2.5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) for 6.0 s. Find its final velocity and displacement.
    Show solution

    \(v=0+(2.5)(6.0)=15\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). \(\Delta x=0+\tfrac12(2.5)(6.0)^2=45\,\mathrm{m}\).

  4. Stopping distance: A vehicle moving at \(+18\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) decelerates uniformly at \(-3.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\). How far does it travel before stopping?
    Show solution

    Use \(v^2=v_0^2+2a\Delta x\). Thus \(0=18^2+2(-3.0)\Delta x\), giving \(\Delta x=54\,\mathrm{m}\).

  5. Free fall: A stone is dropped from rest. How far does it fall in 3.0 s, ignoring air resistance?
    Show solution

    Distance = \(\tfrac12gt^2=\tfrac12(9.81)(3.0)^2\approx44.1\,\mathrm{m}\).

  6. Conceptual: A ball reaches the highest point of its vertical flight. What are its velocity and acceleration at that instant?
    Show solution

    Its instantaneous velocity is zero, but its acceleration is still \(9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) downward, assuming negligible air resistance.

11. Summary and Study Tips

  • Define a coordinate system and sign convention before calculating.
  • Displacement is final position minus initial position; distance is total path length.
  • Average velocity uses displacement, whereas average speed uses distance.
  • On a position–time graph, gradient gives velocity. On a velocity–time graph, gradient gives acceleration and signed area gives displacement.
  • Use the four kinematic equations only when acceleration is constant.
  • For free fall near Earth, acceleration is always downward, even at the highest point.
